Abstract
This study addresses the growing need to strengthen responsible AI awareness among personnel in security-relevant roles in the context of transnational crime. As policing agencies increasingly rely on AI technologies for surveillance, crime detection, and data analysis, ethical challenges such as data privacy, algorithmic bias, and accountability have emerged, highlighting the need for structured and practical training approaches. To address this gap, this study adopts a mixed-methods, design-based research approach, collecting data from 40 survey participants and three semi-structured interviews in the Omani context. Findings revealed that data privacy was the weakest awareness domain (37.5% baseline correct response rate), and that structured AI ethics training is largely absent from participants’ professional environments. Based on these empirical findings, this study proposes two interconnected contributions: the Ethical AI Awareness and Training Framework (EAATF), comprising six sequential principles: Evaluate, Awareness, Apply, Feedback, Reflect, and Update. and the Data-Driven Ethical AI Awareness and Training Model (DDEATM), a personalized, scenario-based training intervention addressing transnational crime contexts including surveillance bias, data privacy, and cross-border accountability. Initial results indicate an improvement in aggregate AI ethics awareness from 76.3% to 80.6% following scenario-based engagement. This study is particularly relevant to the Sultanate of Oman, where advancing AI integration in policing aligns with Oman Vision 2040, supporting innovation, institutional capacity building, and sustainable security governance.
Keywords: responsible AI, AI ethics awareness, law enforcement training, transnational crime, scenario-based learning, DDEATM, Oman Vision 2040

I. Introduction
In the age of Information and modern technologies, Artificial Intelligence (AI), which is a field of Machine Learning (ML), is playing a pivotal role in transforming individuals’ lives toward massive automation. AI must be distinguished from the other kinds of automation, since this technology is capable of learning, adapting, and making decisions (Zhang, 2025). Almost all industries and professional and social domains have witnessed a huge adoption of this technology in their fields, including healthcare, education, agriculture, law enforcement, and policing (Díaz-Rodríguez et al., 2023). The breakthroughs that AI has made in various fields aside, recently, this critical field of computer science has been massively employed in Law Enforcement and Policing, and has proposed several enhancements, such as increased accuracy, speed, and efficiency in areas such as vehicle plate detection using Automated Number Plate Recognition (ANPR), individual identification using Automated Facial Recognition (AFR) technology, and predictive policing (Sorell, 2024; Walczak, 2021; Webb et al., 2024).
The stage and extent of AI adoption in law enforcement in countries vary; nations like the United Kingdom, the US, and Germany are utilizing AI in different areas, including predictive policing to anticipate crimes and their hotspots, facial recognition technologies, and matching DNA in crime scenes with stored DNA profiles, using AI to analyze social networks and open source information to identify possible threats to national security, automated plate detection and etc (Agbadamasi et al., 2025; Lunhol & Torhalo, 2024; Sorell, 2024; Turksen & Kafteranis, n.d.; Webb et al., 2024). Identifying criminals, missing individuals, children, and traffic monitoring, as well as predictive policing, have been made possible in India because of the employment of AI in law enforcement and other sectors (Rani, 2024). Compared to the large implementation of AI in law enforcement in the proposed countries, some of the nations, like Indonesia, Nigeria, and Pakistan, are still in the early and experimental stages of AI implementation, and the law enforcement officials of these countries have positive insights toward the

benefits of AI in law enforcement (Kawu, 2025; Soomro et al., 2026; Zuwanda et al., 2024).
Regardless of the degree of AI utilization and its applications in law enforcement in various nations, AI poses some critical challenges that affect individuals’ lives. Data and algorithmic biases, privacy issues and violations, inaccurate and unfair decisions and inequality, lack of transparency and accountability that leads to black-box operation, can be mentioned as the challenges of lacking an ethical and responsible AI in law enforcement (Webb et al., 2024; Zhang, 2025; Zuwanda et al., 2024). Although some of the advanced regions, like the European Union and the United States, are showing interest in the use of AI and strategies to govern these technologies through funding research projects, policy initiatives, and existing legal frameworks, there is a significant gap among this attention and the actual achievements of trustworthy AI. In the context of the EU, the majority of the funded research projects related to AI in law enforcement have concentrated on the technological aspect rather than on key ethical principles, like human rights, fairness, transparency, accountability, and public participation (Sanz-Urquijo et al., 2023). As for the United States, the current laws, such as the Federal Trade Commission Act, and the proposed Algorithmic Accountability Act, and sector-specific legislation like HIPAA, are insufficient to address the problems of AI systems (Agbadamasi et al., 2025). Thus, it ought to be mentioned that despite the usefulness of these technologies in policing and law enforcement, their outcomes are not necessarily aligned with the main principles of ethical AI systems, which causes some damage to a group of people (Lunhol & Torhalo, 2024).
In the context of the current stage of AI governance in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC) countries, they are in between the early and intermediate governance stages. These nations have national strategies and policy documents; however, most of them rely on soft regulation, referring to strategies, ethical principles, and guidelines rather than detailed and binding AI laws. Additionally, the implementation of AI is uneven across them; some countries, like the UAE and Saudi Arabia, are much more advanced because of

IV. Background
This section introduces the key concepts and terminology that provide the theoretical background for the study.

A. AI In Law Enforcement
Integrating Artificial Intelligence (AI) elements and applications within legal processes, data analysis, and enhancing security measures to improve efficiency in criminal investigation and activities and enhance judicial capabilities without possessing free will or legal personality, while considering compliance with legal standards, refers to AI in law enforcement (Baltrūnienė, 2023; Chucha, 2023).
Employing AI in law enforcement accelerates criminal investigations, automates vehicle plate number and facial recognition, and helps in crime prevention using predictive policing to anticipate crime types or possible crime locations (Walczak, 2021; Webb et al., 2024). A real-world example of utilizing AI in law enforcement can be mentioned as the TRACE project (Turksen & Kafteranis, n.d.), which helped the Law Enforcement Authorities and Financial Intelligence Units identify, track, and document illicit money flows, particularly in money laundering cases. There are other applications of AI in the context of law enforcement, such as criminal recognition in real time using videos captured from surveillance cameras, finding missing individuals, as well as monitoring and controlling traffic (Lunhol & Torhalo, 2024; Rani, 2024).
B. AI-Assisted Vs. Automated Decision-Making
In AI-Assisted Decision Making (AIADM), humans are the final decision makers, and AI technology is employed as a tool to provide recommendations. This aids team performance by utilizing human judgment and reducing reconciliation expenses. In contrast, Automated Decision Making is where AI independently makes all decisions without any human intervention or judgment (Li et al., 2023; Noti & Chen, 2023; Wolczynski et al., 2023).
Due to the sophisticated, dynamic, and highly context-dependent nature of law enforcement, there is a need for an investigator’s knowledge and expertise in the final decision-making. Even with the growth of technology and AI systems in the future, this need remains critical (Nowack et al., 2025). Thus, AI-Assisted Decision Making is more appropriate

C. Responsible and Trustworthy AI
A group of principles, practices, and governance processes that help the design, development, deployment, and use of AI systems in an ethical, secure, accountable, and trustworthy manner is

known as Responsible AI. This type of AI is not only concerned with its accurate operation, but it also attempts to consider the societal values of how it affects people and society, and ensures that the system design respects existing laws and ethical values (What Is Responsible AI?, 2024). D. AI as a Socio-Technical System
In the context of law enforcement, AI raises not only algorithmic and technical concerns but also its outputs may be influenced by developers’ assumptions, policing practices, legal rules, organizational priorities, human judgment, and the wider social context in which the system operates (Goldberg, 2022; Ugwudike, 2022). For instance, most of the AI training datasets and benchmarks are derived from Western-centric data, which results in cultural biases, underrepresentation of non-Western languages and demographics, and the imposition of Western values

onto global users (Zhang, 2025). This is an important matter in law enforcement, since differences in culture, language, behavior, policing practices, and legal systems might affect the interpretation of how suspicious behavior or criminal activity is interpreted.
Hence, an awareness and training framework for AI in law enforcement ought to go beyond preventing bias in datasets; it must also assess the assumptions built into the system, its transparency, institutional objectives, effects on different communities, and the mechanisms available to prevent, correct, and assign responsibility for harmful results.

E. AI Ethics Awareness and Ethical DecisionMaking Competency
AI ethics awareness means understanding the implications of using AI in law enforcement to ensure accountability, transparency and protection of human rights. The reason it is important is because of mitigating risks that are related to AI, like privacy violations and biased decision-making (Masters in Criminal Justice, College of Arts and Sciences, Lamar University, Beaumont, Texas, USA et al., 2024). Thus, there is a difference between AI ethics awareness and general knowledge about AI. To ensure there is a development of responsible AI in law enforcement and to appropriately navigate ethical challenges in AI, awareness of ethical AI is crucial (Khan et al., 2022).
Existence of a clear and structured awareness framework enhances the ethical decision-making competency, because these trainings enable the decision makers to learn more about past mistakes made by these AI systems and, through this deeper comprehension, they can make more consistent ethical decisions (De Cremer & Narayanan, 2023).

G. Transnational Crime and Cross-border AI Use
A group of activities that span across national borders and more than one country and are related to illicit activities is referred to as transnational crime. Involvement of multiple governments and criminal justice systems is required in this kind of crime, making the process of investigation and prosecution much more complicated (What Is Transnational Crime?, n.d.). As previously mentioned, the TRACE project is a great example of how AI-assisted tools can support transnational investigation by analyzing and visualizing complex illicit money flows and patterns across multiple jurisdictions (Turksen & Kafteranis, n.d.).

Scenario-based ethics training can improve decision-making, especially in the context of law enforcement, because it fosters moral evaluation and enhances officer wellness; as a result, the implicit biases will be addressed (Dempsey et al., 2023). This special type of training increases situational awareness and allows officers to develop critical thinking skills useful for communication and ethical decision-making in crisis (Hintze, n.d.; Lavoie et al., 2022).
A data-driven training approach attempts to evaluate the current knowledge level of participants using various methods, including surveys, responses, scenario-based assessments, and interviews, to determine the content and level of training required. This type of training has some positive impacts, such as personalized learning, enhanced learner engagement, demonstrable training effectiveness, and continuous improvements. The use of AI-supported systems in transnational investigations raises a set of problems that do not arise in purely domestic policing. Four are particularly relevant to the Omani context.

The second concerns the legal basis for cooperation between authorities. Oman is a party to the Arab Convention on Combating Information Technology Offences, ratified by Royal Decree No. 5/2015, under which a party may request another to investigate, access, seize, secure, or disclose information technology data located within the territory of the requested party (Sultanate of Oman, 2015; Dominioni, 2023). The third concerns evidential admissibility. An output generated by an AI system in one jurisdiction, using training data and validation standards determined there, may be introduced in proceedings in another. Where the basis of the output cannot be explained to the standard required by the receiving forum, its evidential value is uncertain. This difficulty compounds the explainability problems already identified in domestic policing contexts (Herrewijnen et al., 2024).
The fourth concerns divergent governance regimes. AI systems used in cross-border investigations may be developed under one regulatory framework and deployed under another, with different requirements concerning human oversight, transparency, and permissible data use. In the absence of a harmonized regional framework, officers may be required to act on outputs produced under standards that differ from those applying in their own jurisdiction (Albous et al., 2024).
These problems share a common characteristic: each requires the officer to make a judgment that domestic training does not prepare them to make. This provides the rationale for treating transnational contexts as a distinct training requirement rather than as a variant of domestic practice, and it informs the design of the cross-border accountability scenario within DDEATM.

V. Systematic Literature Review
This section will provide an overview of related papers to this study by highlighting their findings and results and comparing them with other research papers.
A. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria
This paper conducted a systematic literature review that included various papers published between 2018 and 2026, including empirical studies, reviews, and conference papers, and peer-reviewed articles related to responsible AI in law enforcement and transactional crime. The review also considered relevant projects and frameworks proposed or implemented in different countries to provide a broader understanding of the current state of responsible AI in law enforcement from both global and Sultanate of Oman perspectives. This enabled the study to examine the connection between theoretical concepts of responsible AI and their practical application in law enforcement contexts. Non-English, irrelevant to the research focus, non-peer-reviewed, and inaccessible in fulltext papers were excluded from this review.
B. Search Strategy
The literature search was conducted across multiple academic databases and search engines, such as IEEE Xplore, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect, Google Scholar, ResearchGate, BASE (Bielefeld Academic Search Engine), SciSpace, and Semantic Scholar. Various combinations of keywords were utilized related to AI, responsible AI, AI ethics, law enforcement, policing, criminal justice, transactional crime, AI governance, privacy, bias, transparency, accountability and human rights. Initially, simple search strings employing Boolean Operators (AND, OR, NOT) were used to find related papers (e.g., “responsible AI” AND “law enforcement”) , also the filtering features available in some search-engines like Google Scholar was used to filter papers published between 2018 and 2026 to meet mentioned Inclusion criteria. In addition, the reference lists of identified relevant studies were examined to identify further publications related to the research topic.

D. Review Findings and Synthesis
Previous research has emphasized both opportunities and risks related to the use of AI in policing (Webb et al., 2024). As mentioned, AI technology can improve the speed, accuracy, and efficiency of law enforcement activities using applications such as predictive policing, automated image analysis, facial recognition, and traffic monitoring. For instance, Automated Plate Recognition (ANPR) is capable of identifying vehicle plate numbers in

real time, while AI-supported traffic monitoring systems have been used in Delhi, India (Rani, 2024). Automated Facial Recognition technologies can also assist police in identifying individuals of interest and locating missing persons or children (Rani, 2024; Sorell, 2024). Despite these advantages, AI systems may reproduce bias that is contained in the historical datasets, resulting in unfair classification of some people and the over-policing of marginalised communities. However, they stopped using it in 2021, since it was disproportionately classifying some groups as being at high risk (Webb et al., 2024). This paper (Ugwudike, 2022) adds more to the risks caused by these systems by arguing that unfair outcomes are not produced only by historically biased datasets; they may also result from assumptions and theories embedded in the system’s design by developers and creators of these AI systems.
Training plays a pivotal role in having an ethical AI system in law enforcement, since it promotes awareness among the public, law enforcement officials, and IT specialists by highlighting the AI implications and helps to build trust and increase the engagement of society (Brand, 2022; Zuwanda et al., 2024). This paper (Sorell, 2024) examined how the UK police projects are ethically reviewed by using the ALGOCARE checklist that contains questions analyzing whether an AI system is safe, fair, legal, and appropriate before using it. The paper studied two ethics commities including the West Midlands Police Data Ethics Committee and the Biometrics and Forensics Ethics Group. It identified that there is a knowledge gap between police officers who may not fully understand data science, AI limitations, or data protection, and data science and IT specialists who may not fully comprehend policing procedures, legal limits, and police ethics.
Adding to the paper by (Sorell, 2024), which used ALGOCARE practically, this article (Goldberg, 2022) proposed two approaches for evaluating the ethics of AI systems in policing, including top-down and bottomup approaches. The top-down approach starts with established ethical principles and frameworks, like ALGOCARE, and attempts to apply them to a specific ethical issue that arise from that situation. On the other hand, the bottom-up approach begins with identifying ethical issues in a real policing case. Explainability and co-design with police users are also emphasized in this paper to make sure that AI tools are appropriate for operational work. The case-based assessment method proposed in this article supports the need for practical and scenario-based ethics training that teaches police officers

and technical specialists how to recognize ethical risks and evaluate AI systems within a real policing context.
The crucial gap between high-level ethicalAI principles and their practical implementation in real systems was highlighted by this article (Sanderson et al., 2022). Although the responsible AI principles (e.g., fairness, transparency, accountability, etc) are widely recognized, there is a lack of concrete technical translation of these concepts into operational practices. This issue was also reflected in the discussion of (Selbst et al., 2019), which explains that engineering approaches attempt to translate and simplify complex social and ethical problems into mathematical and measurable technical variables, resulting in the implementation of these systems in total abstraction. In areas like law enforcement, this abstraction can create framing and formalism problems. To address the mentioned gap, this paper (Lu et al., 2022) creates a software engineering roadmap for responsible AI. After carefully reviewing 159 primary studies to identify the existing responsible AI solutions, the authors proposed a roadmap containing three main perspectives: governance, development process, and system design. The suggested roadmap indicates that responsible AI must go beyond theoretical concepts and be applied across the full AI lifecycle through ethical requirements, testing, human oversight, monitoring, and accountability. Role-specific ethics training for managers, developers, data scientists, and operators was recommended as well by this paper, which shows the importance of training and awareness. This approach was developed further by(Lu et al., 2023) through reusable practices such as ethical risk assessment, stakeholder engagement, documentation, explainability, testing, human oversight, and continuous monitoring.
Based on the reviewed studies, the literature emphasized that the preparation of ethical concepts ought to occur before AI systems are implemented in risky fields like law enforcement. This study (Rani, 2024) argues that all applications of AI in policing must be supported by a suitable education level, human supervision, strong legal safeguards, and a diverse dataset to reduce the risk of bias and human rights violations. Similarly, this article (Lu et al., 2023) explains that employees need enough and appropriate ethical risk awareness to make responsible choices during the development and use of AI systems.

The context of some advanced countries, like the US (Agbadamasi et al., 2025), shows that there is a need for a dedicated responsible AI framework, ethical safeguards, and professional awareness before deployment, as the existing laws are not sufficient to govern modern AI systems. The development and application of Explainable AI in law enforcement make this need especially clear. Finally, the model was personalized for law-enforcement officers, IT specialists, and general users according to their different roles and training needs.

D. Implications for Transnational Contexts
The accountability divergence reported in Section VII-C acquires additional significance in cross-border settings. Where participants operating within a single institutional environment attributed responsibility variously to the officer, the information-technology function, and to no identifiable party, the introduction of a second jurisdiction adds further candidates: the requesting authority, the authority operating the system, and the entity that supplied the training data. An accountability model that is unsettled domestically is unlikely to resolve when responsibility is distributed across national boundaries, a requirement emphasized in established responsible AI guidance (Artificial Intelligence Toolkit, n.d.; Ethics Guidelines for Trustworthy AI | Shaping Europe’s Digital Future, n.d.; Oswald et al., 2018).
The disclosure constraints reported by P1 and P3 are similarly consequential. Where participants were unable to describe whether digital systems are applied to cross-border cases, the same confidentiality requirements would govern any attempt to reconstruct the decision chain following a harmful outcome in a transnational investigation. This reinforces the argument for embedding accountability content within institutional training rather than relying on external review.
DDEATM addresses these conditions through its cross-border accountability scenario, in which participants respond to an AI system identifying a suspect in another jurisdiction on insufficient evidence. Future development of the model should extend this scenario to incorporate the data-transfer controls and cooperation mechanisms described in Section III-G, so that participants engage with the legal basis for cross-border action rather than with the ethical dimension alone.
E. Implications for EAATF and DDEATM
The integrated findings provide a preliminary empirical basis for EAATF and DDEATM. The comparatively low baseline result for data privacy confirms the relevance of the privacy scenario and indicates that privacy should receive greater

emphasis in future versions of the training. Similarly, uncertainty surrounding responsibility for harmful AIassisted decisions supports including accountability content and further developing the cross-border accountability scenario.
The absence of dedicated AI ethics training among the interview participants also supports the sequential structure of EAATF. The framework begins by evaluating participants’ existing awareness, develops foundational knowledge, and allows participants to apply that knowledge through realistic situations. This structure is informed by established principles of knowledge assessment and progressive learning (Bloom et al -Taxonomy of Educational Objectives, n.d.) and experiential learning (Kolb, 2014). Its Feedback and Reflect principles encourage participants to understand the consequences of their choices (Hattie & Timperley, 2007), while the Update principle allows training content to respond to technological, legal, and operational developments.
Interview participants’ preferences for on-the-job training, simulations, realistic scenarios, workshops, case studies, and online modules support the practical design of DDEATM. These preferences indicate that the proposed format corresponds with the learning needs expressed by participants. Its personalized tracks for law-enforcement officers, IT specialists, and general users also recognize that these groups have different responsibilities and levels of technical and operational knowledge. However, the effectiveness of these tracks must be evaluated through future implementation with larger and more representative samples.
F. Relevance to Oman and the GCC Context
The findings have potential relevance to Oman and similar contexts across the GCC where responsible AI practices and professional training continue to develop. The privacy, accountability, and training gaps identified in this study suggest that transforming high-level ethical principles into practical competency requires targeted and context-sensitive educational interventions. IX. Conclusion

Participants did well in identifying algorithmic bias and critically assessing AI outputs, but data privacy was the weakest area (37.5%), according to the results, which demonstrated inconsistent ethical awareness. EAATF provides six training principles, namely: Evaluate, Awareness, Apply, Feedback, Reflect, and Update, while DDEATM translates them into a seven-component, personalized training intervention for law-enforcement personnel, IT specialists, and general users.
